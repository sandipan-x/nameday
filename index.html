What Is a Name Day, Exactly?

In much of Europe, your name day — the feast day of the saint you were named after — is celebrated with the same enthusiasm many countries reserve for birthdays. Say "Elena" in Greece or "János" in Hungary and people will ask when your name day is, not your birthday. The custom flows from Christian baptism: children were traditionally named after saints, and the saint's feast day became the child's annual celebration. In Orthodox countries the link is even formal — the name day (imeniny) was historically the real celebration, with birthdays a minor affair.

The practical result is a parallel calendar most Americans have never seen. Diaries and wall calendars in Poland, Hungary, Slovakia, Latvia, Sweden, and Greece print a name or two on every single day. Offices keep lists; colleagues bring flowers and cake when your day comes up. If you have European roots and have never celebrated yours, you have been missing a party that much of the continent takes for granted.

How Names Got Their Days

The system grew organically over a thousand years. Early Christians commemorated martyrs on the anniversaries of their deaths — their "birthdays into heaven" — and churches kept lists called martyrologies. As naming children after saints became the norm (in some eras and places it was effectively required), each person's feast day followed from their name. The calendar filled up: popular saints like John, Mary, Peter, and Catherine accumulated multiple feast days across the year, and different regions picked different ones.

That is why there is no single global name-day list. St. Catherine is November 25 in most of the West but April 29 in Italy (Catherine of Siena). St. John has June 24 (his nativity) and December 27 (the Evangelist). National calendars — Sweden's and Finland's are officially published, Latvia's contains over a thousand names — standardize one date per name for civil use. The finder above uses widely recognized Western dates and notes the saint, so you can celebrate confidently and adjust if your family's tradition differs.

Country by Country: How It Is Celebrated

Greece may love name days most: the celebrant's home stays open all day, friends drop in unannounced, and sweets and drinks are served to everyone. Your yiorti is announced with "chronia polla" — many years. Poland's imieniny flip the hosting: the celebrated person throws the party and provides the vodka, while guests bring flowers. In Hungary, name days are so embedded that there are often two per name in the calendar, and coworkers celebrate both.

Sweden and Finland print the official name-day list in every almanac; the royal family's name days are flag-flying days. Latvia publishes one of the world's longest lists, and Latvians abroad still check it. In Bulgaria, name days function as open-house family holidays — St. George's Day (May 6) doubles as a spring festival with lamb and parades. Spain celebrates the onomástica, and Mexico inherited the custom: many Mexicans celebrate their santo alongside or instead of their birthday.

Birthdays vs. Name Days

Why did some cultures elevate the name day above the birthday? Partly theology — the saint's feast connects you to your heavenly patron — and partly practicality: in eras of high infant mortality, baptism (and naming) was the certain, recorded event, while birth dates were fuzzier. The Orthodox tradition made it explicit: your imeniny was your spiritual birthday. Even today, many Greeks will tell you the birthday is for children and the name day is for adults.

The two celebrations have different economics, too. Birthday parties are hosted for you; in several name-day traditions (Poland, Bulgaria, Greece) the celebrant hosts or keeps open house. That makes the name day the more social holiday — less about being celebrated, more about gathering your people. If your calendar only has room for one party a year, much of Europe votes name day.

Names With More Than One Day

Common names are the trickiest. Maria/Mary has the Assumption (August 15), the Holy Name (September 12), the Nativity (September 8), and the Immaculate Conception (December 8), among others — different countries picked different ones. John spans June 24 and December 27. Francis covers October 4 (Assisi) and December 3 (Xavier). Teresa has October 15 (Avila) and September 5 (Calcutta). Anthony has January 17 (the Abbot) and June 13 (Padua). The search above lists every match it finds, so you can pick the one your family uses — or celebrate them all, which some enthusiasts genuinely do.

Orthodox calendars add another layer: many saints' days fall 13 days later than Western ones because they are still reckoned on the Julian calendar. If your family is Greek, Russian, or Serbian Orthodox, check the Orthodox date — your yiorti may be nearly two weeks after the Western listing.

What If Your Name Has No Saint?

Modern names — invented spellings, surnames-as-first-names, names from other religions — often have no feast day at all. Traditions handle this several ways. Some families pick the closest saint's name (a "Vanessa" might borrow St. Anne's day). Some celebrate on All Saints' Day, November 1, the catch-all feast. In Latvia and Estonia, where calendars were expanded deliberately, committees have assigned days to modern names — a charming bureaucratic solution. And some people simply adopt the name day of the saint they feel closest to. The custom is celebratory, not legalistic; nobody checks your baptismal certificate.

Diaspora families often invent the tradition fresh: pick the saint's day, put it in the family calendar, and start a ritual — a special meal, a small gift, a phone call. Within a generation it feels ancient. That is, after all, how all traditions start.

The calendar is, among other things, a map of two thousand years of veneration.

Gifts, Flowers, and Etiquette

Name-day etiquette varies, but the constants are flowers and something sweet. In Poland and Hungary, an odd number of flowers (even numbers are for funerals). In Greece, you do not wait for an invitation — you simply show up, and the host is ready. In offices across Central Europe, the celebrant brings cake or pastries for colleagues. Gifts are modest: a bottle, a book, a small token. The unforgivable sin is forgetting — which is exactly why wall calendars print the names, and why this tool exists.

If you are the celebrant in a hosting tradition, the rule is abundance: more food and drink than seems necessary. Name-day open houses in Greece famously run all day with a rolling cast of visitors. When in doubt, ask a local — but showing up with flowers and congratulations ("Sto lat!" in Polish — "a hundred years!") has never gone wrong.

Frequently asked questions

What is a name day?

A name day is the feast day of the saint you were named after. In much of Europe — Greece, Poland, Hungary, Sweden, Latvia and others — it is celebrated like a second birthday, with flowers, gatherings and congratulations.

How do I find my name day?

Search your first name in the finder above. It returns the traditional date and the saint behind it, and marks it on the month calendar. If your name has several saints, pick the date your family or country observes.

Is a name day the same as a birthday?

No. Your birthday marks your birth; your name day marks your patron saint's feast. In countries like Greece and Russia the name day was historically the bigger celebration, and many adults there still treat it that way.

What if my name has no saint or no name day?

Common solutions: celebrate on All Saints' Day (November 1), adopt the closest saint's day, or use your country's official calendar — Latvia and Estonia have assigned days to many modern names. The custom is celebratory, not strict.

Why do some names have more than one name day?

Popular saints have multiple feast days (John: June 24 and December 27; Francis: October 4 and December 3), and different countries standardized different ones. The finder lists every match so you can choose your tradition.

Do Orthodox name days fall on different dates?

Often, yes. Many Orthodox churches reckon saints' days on the Julian calendar, about 13 days behind the Gregorian dates shown here. Greek, Russian and Serbian families should check their church calendar for the exact day.
